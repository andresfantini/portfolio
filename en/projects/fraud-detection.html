Credit Card Fraud Detection

The challenge

Detecting potential credit card fraud presents a particular challenge: fraudulent transactions constitute a minority class, which makes them difficult to identify using conventional classification methods. The objective was to analyze the patterns present in the data and develop predictive models capable of identifying potentially fraudulent transactions, while evaluating their performance in the face of class imbalance.

Approach & contribution

As part of a diploma program offered by the Faculty of Mathematics, Astronomy, and Physics (FaMAF) at the National University of Córdoba—a program recognized for its academic rigor and the depth of its training in programming, mathematics, statistics, econometrics, machine learning, and big data analysis—I spent a year developing a credit card fraud prevention project. The program was under the academic direction of Dr. Francisco Tamarit, a leading Argentine authority on research into neural networks and machine learning. The project was based on a dataset containing 1,000,000 records and eight columns, for which I applied in-depth techniques in exploratory data analysis, predictive modeling, and model optimization.

  • I explored distributions, correlations, and transactional patterns, paying particular attention to class imbalance.
  • I organized preprocessing within pipelines and compared unweighted, class-weighted, and training-only undersampling approaches.
  • I compared Logistic Regression, Decision Trees, and Random Forest, selecting hyperparameters with cross-validated Average Precision.
  • I selected the decision threshold using F2 on a separate calibration set and documented false positives and false negatives on 200,000 held-out simulated transactions.
In fraud analytics, evaluating the consequences of a mistake is as important as measuring predictive performance.

What this demonstrates

This project demonstrates exploratory analysis, supervised classification, cross-validation, hyperparameter tuning, and reproducible evaluation. It also demonstrates my ability to critically review earlier work, correct methodology, and communicate applicability limits.

Evidence & scope

The 2026 reassessment used a public simulated dataset of 1,000,000 records. On 200,000 held-out transactions, the model detected 17,476 of 17,481 simulated fraud cases (99.9714% recall), with five false negatives and no false positives. The model was frozen before final testing. These results do not measure performance on real banking transactions or imply deployment. The original 2022 results are kept separate.
